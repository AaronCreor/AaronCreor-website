Building IdentifAI: What Your Browser Gives Away

Published Nov 4, 2026

All Posts Home

Why I Built It

A website can learn quite a bit about your browser before you type anything into it. Your language, timezone, screen settings, browser version, and graphics capabilities are all available to varying degrees. I wanted to see those details together and find out how often another browser would look the same.

That became IdentifAI. I took inspiration from AmIUnique, particularly the way it puts your own results in front of you and lets you compare individual attributes against a dataset.

Putting the Demo First

I originally approached IdentifAI as a device intelligence and fraud detection project, with an SDK, API keys, and a dashboard. Those parts still exist, but I found myself much more interested in the public fingerprint test.

I changed the homepage so the test runs when you open it. You see your browser attributes, your connection IP, and how common each available value is among the saved samples. I also cut a lot of copy from the site. The report already gives people something to explore, and it was getting buried under explanations and marketing language.

Making the Comparisons Useful

The report separates the full fingerprint match from the frequency of each attribute. Lots of browsers might share your preferred language. Fewer might share your particular combination of language, timezone, screen settings, and rendering results. Seeing both makes it easier to understand how ordinary settings can add up to a recognizable browser.

The counts matter here. A result that appears once in a small dataset does not tell you how rare it is across the internet. I show the number of comparable samples alongside the percentages. Missing or blocked attributes are marked unavailable rather than treated as measurements the browser actually returned.

A Couple of Details That Took Work

One early deployment showed everyone's IP as 127.0.0.1. The application was reading the connection from the hosting proxy instead of the visitor address. Fixing that meant configuring which proxies the server should trust when reading forwarded addresses.

Even displaying the Windows version needed some care. A user agent can report Windows 10 on a Windows 11 machine. Some browsers provide additional version hints, while others do not. The report uses those hints when available and shows the ambiguity when it cannot distinguish the versions. There is no benefit in displaying a more specific answer than the browser supports.

Looking at the Dataset

The app uses Node.js, Express, server-rendered EJS pages, and MySQL. Browser checks run in JavaScript, and Firebase handles account sign-in for the dashboard. The public test works without an account.

Samples are saved automatically for comparison, with a one-year retention period after their latest update. The comparison dataset stores keyed hashes of attribute values. Repeat visits with the same participation token update that browser's sample.

I added a research page to look beyond a single report. It charts how many samples share a fingerprint and which attributes vary most. The results describe the browsers that have used the test. They do not establish someone's identity, and a saved sample is not necessarily a different person.

What I Want to Explore Next

I would like to understand more about how fingerprints change over time. A browser update, a different display, or a privacy setting can change the result. A fingerprint being distinctive on one visit and staying recognizable across several visits are separate questions.

For now, you can try the test at identifai.ca and look through what your own browser reports. That is the part I wanted to build around: being able to inspect the actual values instead of just reading that browser fingerprinting exists.
